## Positive reinforcement in plain language

Positive reinforcement means that something meaningful happens after a behaviour, making that behaviour more likely to happen again. The “positive” part means that something is added; it does not mean that the child’s behaviour is judged as good or bad.

For example, a child may hand over a picture to ask for a break. If the break follows promptly, the child learns that this form of communication works. The important outcome is not compliance. It is a useful skill that gives the child a clearer way to influence what happens around them.

## Reinforcement must be individual

What feels rewarding to one child may be neutral or unpleasant to another. A reinforcer could be access to a favourite activity, movement, attention from a trusted person, a sensory experience, a small object or simply a pause from a difficult task.

Preferences also change. A therapist should keep observing, offer choices and avoid assuming that yesterday’s favourite item will still be motivating today. The child’s response—not the adult’s intention—shows whether something is functioning as reinforcement.

## What thoughtful use can look like

### Start with a meaningful skill

A goal should improve participation, communication, safety, independence or access to enjoyable experiences. “Looking typical” is not a meaningful goal by itself. The reason for teaching a skill should be understandable to the child’s caregivers and, whenever possible, to the child.

### Make the connection clear

When a skill is new, reinforcement usually needs to follow quickly and reliably. Long delays make it harder to understand what led to the positive outcome. Adults can use simple, specific language such as, “You asked for help—here I am.”

### Keep the task achievable

Practice should begin at a level where the child can succeed. Prompts may help at first, but they should be reduced deliberately so the child does not become dependent on adult cues. Small steps allow the therapist to reinforce genuine progress rather than wait for perfect performance.

### Review and adjust

Data can help answer practical questions: Is the skill becoming more frequent? Does it happen with different people and in different places? Is the child becoming more independent? Numbers should support professional judgement, not replace attention to comfort, assent and context.

## Reinforcement is not bribery

A bribe is commonly offered during a conflict to stop a problem immediately. A reinforcement plan is prepared in advance: the target skill is clear, the outcome is predictable and adults respond consistently. The aim is learning, not bargaining under pressure.

That distinction does not make every reinforcement plan automatically appropriate. If a child is exhausted, overwhelmed, in pain or trying to escape something unsafe, increasing the reward may miss the real problem. Behaviour always needs context.

## Questions parents can ask

- What useful skill are we teaching, and why does it matter to my child?
- How were possible reinforcers chosen?
- How will my child be offered meaningful choices?
- What signs would show that the plan is stressful or no longer effective?
- How will prompts and extra rewards be reduced over time?
- How will we know whether the skill is useful outside therapy sessions?

## The practical takeaway

Positive reinforcement is most helpful when it supports a worthwhile skill, reflects the child’s current preferences and is part of a transparent plan. Families should be able to see what is being taught, why it matters, how progress is reviewed and how the child’s wellbeing remains central.

This article provides general educational information, not an individual assessment or treatment plan. Decisions for a particular child should be made with an appropriately qualified professional who can understand the full context.
